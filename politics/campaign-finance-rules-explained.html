Campaign Finance Rules: What the Paperwork Actually Says

Campaign finance is not a mystery. It is a stack of forms, deadlines, and limits. Here is how to read it without getting sold a story.

5 min read Politics

Start With the Filing

Every federal candidate has to file reports with the Federal Election Commission. Those reports list money raised, money spent, and who gave what above a certain threshold. The threshold and the schedule are set in law and in FEC rules, not by the campaign. If you want to know where a candidate's money came from, the filing is the first place to look. Not the press release. The filing.

The Limits Are Set in Code

Individual contribution limits to federal candidates are set in statute and adjusted over time. They are not whatever a campaign says on a website. The same goes for what a party committee can take and what a political action committee can take. Worked example: if a limit is set at a number the FEC publishes, a donor giving twice that to the same candidate in the same election is a question worth asking. You can check the number yourself against the FEC's published limits. Do not take my number or anyone else's on faith.

Disclosure Has Gaps

Small-dollar donations below the itemization threshold do not get listed by name. That is by design, and it is not automatically sinister. But it means a campaign can raise a lot of money in small chunks and you will not see every name. Independent spending is a separate track with its own reporting. When you see a big number in a headline, ask: is this money given to the campaign, or spent by an outside group? Those are different buckets with different rules.

The Enforcement Question

Rules only matter if someone checks. The FEC handles federal complaints, and its process is slow and often political. State races run on state rules, which vary widely. So the honest answer to "is this legal?" is usually "check the filing and check the rule." A complaint is not a finding. An investigation is not a conviction. Keep those separate in your head.

What to Watch For

Three habits help. One: read the actual report, not the summary. Two: separate candidate money from outside money. Three: note the date. A donation reported in one quarter may have been made earlier. Timelines get blurred in coverage, sometimes on purpose. If a story leans hard on a number, find the filing it came from. If there is no filing, treat the number as a claim.

The Bottom Line

Campaign finance is a paperwork story. The forms are public. The limits are published. The gaps are known. You do not need a law degree to follow the money, but you do need to read the source documents and keep the buckets straight.

General Information
