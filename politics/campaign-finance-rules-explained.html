Campaign Finance Rules: The Basics, Minus the Spin

Campaign finance law is not one rulebook. It is a stack of statutes, agency rules, and court decisions. Here is the lay of the land without the noise.

5 min read Politics

Who writes the rules

Congress passes the statutes. The Federal Election Commission writes and enforces rules for federal races. States run their own systems for state and local contests. Courts can strike rules down or narrow them. That means the answer to "is this allowed?" depends on the office, the donor, and the timing. Ask three questions: federal or state, candidate or independent group, and when the money moves.

Contribution limits and disclosure

Direct contributions to a candidate are capped and must be reported. Money spent independently of a campaign lives under different limits. Disclosure is the backbone of the system: donors above certain thresholds get named in public filings. The exact dollar caps and thresholds change, so check the current FEC charts or your state election office. Do not trust a number from a meme. Look it up at the source.

The independent spending split

After the major court decisions, independent spending by outside groups grew. The legal line is coordination. If a group spends independently of a campaign, different rules apply than if it coordinates. That line is where most enforcement fights happen. A worked example: if a group runs ads and never talks to the campaign, it may fall under independent rules. If staff share strategy with the campaign, that is a different problem. The facts decide it, not the label on the group.

Dark money and the disclosure gap

Some nonprofit groups can spend on politics without naming their donors, depending on their tax status and how much political activity they run. That is the "dark money" debate. Supporters cite free speech and donor privacy. Critics cite transparency. Both sides are arguing about the same gap in the rules. If you want to follow the money, start with public filings and read the footnotes.

Enforcement is slow and uneven

The FEC is structured to be bipartisan, which also means it can deadlock. Complaints can take years. State boards vary widely in staffing and appetite. So the practical reality: many violations get caught by reporters and watchdogs before regulators act. That is not a reason to ignore the rules. It is a reason to treat compliance as a process, not a one-time check.

What to watch next

Watch three things. First, court rulings that redraw the lines. Second, state legislatures tightening or loosening their own systems. Third, disclosure rules for online ads, which keep evolving. If you run a campaign, a PAC, or a nonprofit that touches politics, build a paper trail and get a lawyer who knows your jurisdiction. Cheap insurance.
