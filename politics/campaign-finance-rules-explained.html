Campaign Finance Rules: The Basics, Without the Spin

Campaign finance rules decide who can give, how much, and who gets to know. Here is the short version.

5 min read Politics

The Core Idea

Campaign finance law has one job: make money in politics visible and bounded. Disclosure is the backbone. Donors above certain thresholds get named in public filings. Limits cap direct contributions to candidates. The rules differ by level — federal races follow one rulebook, state and local races follow another. Don't assume a state rule matches the federal one.

Who Enforces What

At the federal level, the Federal Election Commission handles candidate committees and political committees. The FEC is a six-member body, and its structure means enforcement can stall when members split. State election boards run their own shops. Local races often fall to county or city clerks. If you want to check a filing, start with the agency that covers that office, not the one you saw on the news.

The Committees

Money rarely moves straight from a person to a campaign. It moves through committees. Candidate committees take direct contributions. Party committees coordinate at arm's length. Political action committees pool money from members or donors. Super PACs can raise and spend without limit but cannot coordinate with candidates. Each type has its own rules on sources and reporting. Mixing them up is how people get confused.

Disclosure and Timing

Reports are filed on schedules — quarterly, monthly, or pre-election, depending on the committee and jurisdiction. The gap between a donation and its public appearance can be weeks. That lag matters. A headline about a big donor may reflect money given well before the report. Read the filing date before you read the story. Worked example: if a committee files quarterly and a donation lands the day after a filing deadline, it may not show up publicly for roughly three months. Check the actual schedule for that committee.

The Gray Areas

Independent expenditures, issue ads, and dark money groups sit in the murky middle. Some spending is designed to influence an election without saying "vote for" anyone. Courts have shaped these lines over decades, and the rules keep shifting. When you see a claim about coordination or a loophole, ask three questions: who spent the money, what did the ad actually say, and which agency has jurisdiction. If the answer is fuzzy, the claim probably is too.

What to Watch

Watch enforcement, not just the statutes. A rule that is not enforced is a suggestion. Watch the FEC's deadlocks, state board staffing, and disclosure portals. Watch whether filings are searchable. Public access is the real test of any campaign finance regime.

General Information Only

This is general information, not legal, tax, or financial advice. Campaign finance rules vary by jurisdiction and change often. Confirm specifics with a licensed attorney or the election agency that covers the race you care about before acting on anything here.
