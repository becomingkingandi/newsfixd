Campaign Finance Rules: The Basics Without the Spin

Campaign finance law is not one rulebook. It is a stack of them. Here is the short version of how the money moves and who watches it.

5 min read Politics

The Two Pots

Most campaign money falls into two pots. Direct contributions go straight to a candidate's committee. They are capped and must be disclosed. Independent spending is money spent to support or oppose a candidate without coordinating with the campaign. It has different rules and, in many cases, different disclosure. Keep those two pots separate in your head. Most confusion comes from mixing them.

Who Enforces What

At the federal level, the Federal Election Commission handles candidate committees, parties, and political action committees. The FEC is a six-member body, and by design it can deadlock. State and local races run on state law, and those rules vary widely. A rule that is fine in one state can be a violation next door. There is no single national answer.

Disclosure Is the Backbone

The core idea is simple. Voters should be able to see who gave and who spent. Committees file regular reports listing donors above a threshold and itemizing spending. The reports are public. The catch is timing. Reports lag reality, so a big check can land before anyone sees it on a filing. Disclosure is a flashlight, not a live feed.

The Gray Areas

Three places get fuzzy. First, coordination: campaigns cannot coordinate with independent spenders, but the line between talking and not talking is contested. Second, issue ads: speech that names no candidate can fall outside some rules. Third, dark money: groups organized under certain tax sections can spend on politics without naming their donors. Each area has been litigated for years. None is settled.

A Worked Example You Can Check

Say a donor wants to give to a candidate and also fund an independent ad. Step one: check the direct contribution limit for that office and election cycle, then confirm the committee reports it. Step two: check whether the independent group is registered and what it must disclose. Step three: confirm the group does not coordinate with the campaign. The exact limits and forms change by office and cycle, so look them up at the FEC or your state election office. Do not take a number from a headline.

What to Watch

Watch enforcement, not just statutes. A rule that is never enforced is a suggestion. Watch court rulings, because they rewrite the boundaries. And watch state legislatures, because most of the money in politics moves in races that federal law does not touch. If you want to follow the money, follow the filings.

General Information, Not Advice

This article is general information. It is not legal, tax, or financial advice, and it is not personalized to you. Campaign finance rules differ by jurisdiction and change often. If you are giving, spending, or running for office, confirm the specifics with a licensed attorney or your election commission before you act.
