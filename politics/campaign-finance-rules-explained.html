Campaign Finance Rules: What They Actually Do

Campaign finance law is not one rule. It is a stack of rules about who can give, how much, and who has to tell the public. Here is the short version.

5 min read Politics

The Basics

Campaign finance rules govern money in elections. They cover contributions, spending, and disclosure. Three questions do most of the work: Who gave it? How much? Did anyone report it? Federal campaigns answer to the Federal Election Commission. State and local races answer to state election boards. The rules differ by level and by jurisdiction. Do not assume a federal rule applies to a city council race.

Contribution Limits

Limits cap how much one person or group can give directly to a candidate. The caps exist to reduce the risk that a single donor buys influence. Here is a worked example you can check: if a limit were set at a few thousand dollars per election, a donor giving that amount is within the rule. The same donor giving ten times that is not. The exact number is set by law and adjusted over time. Look it up for your race and cycle. Do not take a number from a headline.

Independent Spending

Outside groups can spend on elections without giving directly to a campaign. That spending is supposed to be independent. It cannot be coordinated with the candidate. The catch is the word independent. Coordination rules are where a lot of the legal fighting happens. If spending is truly independent, different limits can apply. If it is not, the rules tighten. This is not a loophole you can self-diagnose. It is a legal question.

Disclosure Is the Real Check

Limits get the attention. Disclosure does the work. Reports show who gave, how much, and where the money went. That paper trail is how the public and the press audit the system. Dark money is the problem case. Money that moves through groups that do not have to name their donors is hard to trace. The fix is not always a lower limit. Sometimes it is a better reporting rule.

Enforcement and Gray Areas

Rules only matter if someone enforces them. The FEC and state boards handle complaints. Enforcement is slow and often political. That is a feature of the design, not a bug. Gray areas are common: earmarked contributions, joint fundraising, in-kind help, and social media ads. When in doubt, campaigns ask counsel. They do not guess.

What to Watch

Watch three things. First, disclosure thresholds. Second, coordination rules for outside groups. Third, enforcement staffing and penalties. Changes in any of those shift how money flows. Read the actual filing, not the press release. The filing is public. Use it.

General Information Only

This is general information, not legal, tax, or financial advice. Campaign finance rules vary by jurisdiction and change often. If you are giving, spending, or running for office, confirm the specifics with a licensed attorney or the relevant election board before you act.
