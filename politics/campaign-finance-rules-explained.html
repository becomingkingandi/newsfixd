Campaign Finance Rules: What They Actually Say

Campaign finance law is long, technical, and full of exceptions. Here is the short version of how it works, who watches it, and where the lines blur.

5 min read Politics

The Basic Split

Campaign money breaks into two lanes. Lane one is money given directly to a candidate's committee. That money is capped and disclosed. Lane two is independent spending — money spent to support or oppose a candidate but not coordinated with them. That lane has looser limits and, in some cases, no public donor trail. Most fights in this area are about the border between those lanes.

Who Enforces What

At the federal level, the Federal Election Commission handles disclosure and civil enforcement. The FEC is a six-member body, and by design it can deadlock. Criminal cases can go to the Department of Justice. States run their own systems for state and local races, and those rules vary a lot. A donor who is fine in one state can be in violation in the next.

Disclosure Is the Backbone

The core idea is simple: voters should be able to see who is paying. Committees file periodic reports listing donors above a threshold. The threshold and the reporting calendar are set by statute and regulation, not by vibes. If you want to check a claim about a race, the filings are public. Read the actual report before believing a headline about it.

The Gray Zones

Three areas cause most confusion. First, coordination: telling a super PAC what ad to run can turn an independent expenditure into an illegal contribution. Second, issue ads: messaging that stops short of explicit advocacy can fall outside some rules. Third, dark money: nonprofits that spend on politics may not have to name their donors. None of these are loopholes in the cartoon sense. They are contested legal lines, and courts keep redrawing them.

A Worked Example You Can Check

Say a donor wants to give to a candidate and also fund an outside ad. The direct gift is capped and reported. The outside ad is a separate transaction with separate rules and a separate paper trail. Now say the donor texts the campaign manager the ad script. That text can change the legal character of the spending. The numbers in any real case depend on the statute and the filing. The principle is what matters: the paper trail and the coordination question decide the outcome.

What to Watch

Watch enforcement, not just statutes. A rule that is not enforced is a suggestion. Watch state legislatures, which move faster than Congress on this. And watch court rulings, which have reshaped this field more than any single bill in recent decades. Read the filings. Follow the money. Skip the outrage.
