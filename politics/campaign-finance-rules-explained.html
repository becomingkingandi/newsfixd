Campaign Finance Rules: What They Actually Do

Campaign finance law is not one rule. It is a stack of rules about who can give, how much, and who has to report it. Here is the working version.

5 min read Politics

The Basic Split

Campaign money comes in two main flavors: money given directly to a candidate's committee, and money spent outside that committee. Direct contributions are capped. Outside spending is not capped, but it comes with disclosure rules and a ban on coordinating with the campaign. That split is the whole game. Most arguments about campaign finance are really arguments about where that line sits.

Contribution Limits

Federal law sets limits on how much an individual or committee can give to a candidate per election. Those numbers get adjusted over time, so check the current figures with the Federal Election Commission rather than trusting a number you heard once. Here is a worked example you can verify: if the per-election limit were $3,000, a donor giving the max in a primary and a general election would give $6,000 total to that candidate. Run the real current limit through the same math and you have your answer.

Disclosure Is the Backbone

Most of the system runs on paperwork. Committees file reports listing donors above a threshold, and those reports are public. Disclosure does not stop money. It makes money visible. When people say a rule is 'working,' they usually mean the reports are filed and the public can read them. When they say it is broken, they usually mean the money moved somewhere the reports do not reach.

Outside Groups and the Coordination Line

Independent expenditure groups can spend unlimited amounts on ads and mail, as long as they do not coordinate with the campaign. 'Coordination' is the pressure point. The rules try to define it, and campaigns and outside groups try to stay just inside the line. This is where most enforcement fights happen. It is also where the law is least intuitive to a normal reader.

What the Rules Do Not Do

They do not cap total spending in a race. They do not stop wealthy donors from giving to outside groups. They do not guarantee fast enforcement. The FEC is a bipartisan body, and deadlocks happen. So a rule on paper and a rule in practice can look different. Keep that gap in mind whenever you read a headline about a 'loophole.'

Why It Matters to You

Campaign finance rules shape which messages you see and who paid for them. The disclosure reports are public. If you care about a race, pull the filings and look at the top donors and the outside groups. That is the check the system is built to give you. It takes an afternoon, not a law degree.
