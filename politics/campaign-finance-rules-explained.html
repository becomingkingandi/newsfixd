Campaign Finance Rules: What They Actually Say

Campaign finance law is not one rule. It is a stack of rules from different eras, some strict, some full of holes. Here is the working version.

5 min read Politics

The Basic Split

Campaign money comes in two main flavors: money given directly to a candidate's committee, and money spent by outside groups. Direct contributions are capped and disclosed. Outside spending has looser limits, and in some cases the donors are not named publicly. That split is the whole game. Most arguments about campaign finance are really arguments about which side of that line a dollar lands on.

Contribution Limits

Federal law caps how much an individual can give a candidate per election, and how much can go to a party committee. The caps are adjusted over time, so check the current figures with the Federal Election Commission rather than trusting an old number. As a worked example: if the per-election limit were $3,000 and you gave $3,000 to a primary and $3,000 to the general, that is $6,000 to one candidate — a figure you can verify against the FEC's published limits. Corporations and unions cannot give directly to federal candidates. They can give through political action committees.

Disclosure

Committees above a certain size must file regular reports listing who gave and how much was spent. This is the part that does the most work. Disclosure does not stop money; it makes money visible. The gaps matter. Small donors below an itemization threshold are not named. Some nonprofit groups that spend on politics do not have to name their donors at all. That is where 'dark money' comes from — not a loophole in the classic sense, but a legal category.

Outside Spending

Independent expenditure groups can spend unlimited amounts to support or oppose a candidate, as long as they do not coordinate with the campaign. That coordination line is the pressure point. In practice, the line is fuzzy, and enforcement is slow. Super PACs must disclose donors. Other entities, depending on their tax status, may not. When you see a big ad buy, the first question is not 'how much' but 'what kind of group paid for it.'

State and Local Rules

Federal rules are only half the picture. States set their own limits, disclosure schedules, and enforcement. Some states are tighter than the federal system; some are looser. Local races often have their own rules on top. If you are tracking a specific race, read the state election commission's rules, not just the federal ones.

What This Means for You

If you give money, know which bucket it goes into and whether your name will show up. If you follow politics, check the filing, not the press release. The FEC and state commissions publish the actual reports. Read them. They are dull and they are the record.
